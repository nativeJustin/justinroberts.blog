If there's one metric I care about during a software implementation, it's time to value (TTV).

I don't necessarily care how quickly we can get an entire company live. I care how quickly we can get somebody at that company using our product to solve a real business problem.

When I think about time to value, I'm looking at the time between when a customer signs a contract and when they first start using the product to solve that problem.

They bought your software for a reason. Maybe they're spending too much time managing compliance, have inefficient business processes, or want their employees spending more time on things that actually generate revenue.

Every week that goes by without them using your product is another week they're dealing with the problems they paid you to solve.

## Implementation is a continuation of discovery

One of the things I've noticed working with customers is how large the gap can be between leadership's understanding of a problem and the reality of the people on the front lines dealing with it.

I think leadership often has the best intentions. They want to solve problems for their employees and free them up to focus on other areas of the business. But they don't always have a complete understanding of the processes they're trying to improve.

This is where something like the Five Whys becomes useful.

A lot of my conversations with customers look something like this:

- Why do you guys do it this way?
- What outcome are you trying to achieve?
- What would happen if we did it differently?
- Why wouldn't that work for your business?

Sometimes a customer has an idea of how something should be built, and I disagree with them. Part of my job is to challenge them on that and explain why I think there's a better way of achieving the outcome they're looking for.

I don't want to spend time replicating an inefficient business process just because that's how they've always done it.

I look at implementation as a continuation of discovery. You're learning how the business operates as a system, where the bottlenecks are, what constraints exist, and where your product can provide the most value.

Your understanding of a business when the contract is signed is rarely going to be as good as your understanding of it after working alongside the people who operate it.

## Crawl, walk, run

I started thinking more about this approach while working on some larger implementations with pilot customers.

Taking on an entire business at once is a beast. There's a lot to build, a lot of people to work with, and a lot of assumptions you're making along the way.

At some point, I started asking myself why we were waiting to get the product in front of people when we could be getting feedback much earlier.

Why wait three to six months to find out that we've been solving the wrong problem?

Some of the customers I work with have smaller divisions within their business, and I like to use those as an entry point. Let's build out what we need to build for that division and get somebody at the company using the product.

This allows me to take more of a forward-deployed approach. I can understand problems at a more granular level, work closely with the people experiencing them, and quickly iterate on their feedback.

I can have a conversation with somebody about a problem, make a change overnight, and get their feedback the next day.

It's much easier to work this way when you're not managing feedback from an entire organization.

Consider two hypothetical customers.

Customer A has a single division using the product within 30 days and gradually rolls it out to the rest of the business over six months. Customer B spends six months implementing the product before rolling it out company-wide.

From a project completion standpoint, both implementations took six months.

But Customer A has spent five of those months actually using the product. We've had time to uncover problems, improve workflows, and build familiarity with the software.

Customer B is just beginning that process, except now they're doing it across the entire organization.

## Early value creates momentum

I think about this a lot like weight loss.

When you start dieting and see those first five or ten pounds come off, it motivates you. You see that what you're doing is working, and it makes you want to continue investing the time and effort.

I think software adoption works similarly.

Once people start seeing the value of the product, they begin thinking beyond the original scope of what you were trying to solve.

How could we use this for another process? Could another division benefit from this? What else could we be doing with this product?

It also gives people time to get comfortable with change.

A lot of people like doing things the way they've always done them. When leadership introduces a significant change to how somebody does their job, there's naturally going to be some resistance.

People want to understand why they're being asked to change something and what the benefit is going to be for them.

Getting the product into their hands early gives you an opportunity to demonstrate that rather than just talk about it.

## Building trust with the customer

Having good champions within an organization, understanding the company's hierarchy, and building relationships with the people using your product all matter during an implementation.

When you're working alongside somebody, understanding their problems, and demonstrating that you can make changes quickly, they begin to trust you. They value your opinion, and you earn more credibility when you challenge something about their existing process.

You're not just some guy who shows up to a weekly meeting and gives them a status update. You're the guy in the trenches with them figuring these things out.

I think having a sense of urgency and taking ownership of your work goes a long way here.

## Building the right things

There is a fine line with this approach, though.

Working closely with a customer and iterating quickly doesn't mean you should build everything they ask for. You also have to think about what you're doing to your own product.

When I'm looking at solving a problem for a customer, there are a few things I want to consider:

- Is this a problem other customers are likely to encounter?
- Can I build this in a way that makes it reusable?
- How does building this benefit our own business?
- Am I solving a broader problem or building something that's siloed to this particular customer?

This is where I think a lot of good product ideas come from. You're seeing how businesses actually use your software and uncovering problems that may not have been apparent when the product was originally built.

It takes critical thinking and creativity to take a problem that's specific to one customer and develop a solution that's applicable to many.

## Measuring what actually matters

Every customer is a snowflake.

What's valuable to one customer isn't necessarily going to be valuable to another, even if they're using the same product to solve similar problems.

I think it's important during discovery to establish some baseline metrics with your customer.

How long does this business process currently take? How many people are involved? How much is their time worth to the business? What would they rather be spending that time doing?

If you understand those things going into an implementation and can measure them again once the product is being used, you have a much clearer story to tell about the value your product is providing.

But you have to understand what your customer actually cares about. Making assumptions about what's valuable to them without having those conversations is a failure on your part to understand their business.

Implementation is also project management. You're responsible for the timelines, the deliverables, the strategy, and ultimately the success of that customer.

I don't think there needs to be some rigid framework for how you get a customer from A to Z. How you get there is going to look different for every business you work with.

At the end of an implementation, I want to know whether we're solving the business problems we set out to solve when we signed the contract. Are people able to focus more on the things their job actually requires of them? Are we freeing people up to do things that generate revenue for the business?

I don't want to wait until the end of an implementation to find out whether we've built the right thing. I want to know that as early as possible.

That's why I optimize for time to value.
